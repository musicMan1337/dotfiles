# 8 Bit Mammoth: Voice & Tone

How the band sounds in writing. The voice should feel like a real person who's in the band, talking about their gig, not like a venue's PR department, not like a jazz festival program note.

## The target

**Warm, relaxed, lit-up about what we do.** A friend telling you their band is playing this weekend, and they're stoked about it. Confident without being formal. Nerdy-affectionate about the source material without making it the whole bit. Excited to play, happy you're coming.

If the copy could plausibly be the opening of a casual social caption *or* the body of a venue event page with only minor edits, you've hit the register.

## What to lean into

- **Plain, conversational language.** Write how people actually talk about their gigs. "We play video game music as funk and jazz" beats "an ensemble that arranges video game music in the funk and jazz idioms." Drop the writerly upgrade.
- **Energy and warmth.** Enthusiasm is allowed. Exclamations are allowed in moderation. Phrases like "come hang," "catch us," "we're playing," "stoked to," "favorite tunes": all on the table when they fit. The copy should sound like the band actually wants to play the show.
- **Specific via craft and feel, not via titles.** Concrete details about *what the band does to the music* ("horn arrangements," "deep pocket," "open grooves," "room to solo") carry weight without name-dropping IPs.
- **Confidence without bragging.** Describe what we do, not how good we are. The seriousness comes through in the doing, not in claims about the doing.
- **Affection for the source.** It's okay, encouraged even, to say we love this music. The trick is to say it like a person says it, not like a press release. "Music we love," "soundtracks we grew up on," "tunes we all love": these work. "Reverently arranged" doesn't. "We all love" is a particularly good move: it lands the band's collective enthusiasm, not just the writer's.
- **Nostalgia is a welcome theme.** The shared-memory hook of game music is a real part of the pitch and shouldn't be sanitized out. Phrases like "that sweet nostalgia beat," "the songs you grew up with," "soundtracks we grew up on" pull readers in and aren't cringe. The trick is to use them as honest emotional anchors, not as sales hooks. Say it because it's true, not because it sells.
- **Vibe words over craft listing.** "Cool groove," "go hard," "slinky," "sweet," "deep pocket": these casual feel-descriptors usually beat detailed craft enumeration ("the horns dig in and the solos run long"). When the copy already has warmth and energy, don't append a craft-detail clause that re-states the obvious. The vibe is doing the work; let it.

## What to actively avoid

Only what's actually load-bearing: these are the moves we've confirmed kill the voice.

- **Naming specific songs, games, or IPs unless the user explicitly asks.** Default copy stays general: "video game music," "soundtracks," "game themes," "the songs you grew up with." Do not name *Cowboy Bebop*, *The Legend of Zelda*, "Tank!", Stickerbrush Symphony, Mario Kart, etc. unless the user requests it for that draft. When the user asks for touchstones, then `repertoire.md` is on the table.
- **Naming individual band members.** Always collective. "We," "the band," "the horn section." If the user wants a featured player named, they'll say so and provide the name.
- **Snobby positioning.** Four moves to avoid: (a) positioning the band against other approaches ("rather than as novelty," "not a covers act"), (b) flattering the audience's taste ("for the listener who appreciates," "the kind of room that expects"), (c) quietly apologizing for the source ("music that happens to come from games"), (d) listing craft credentials as a defensive flex ("the charts are real, the solos are live, the band means it"). The last one is the sneakiest: it looks like description but reads as proving we're serious. Just describe the show in plain language.
- **Stiff "writerly" upgrades.** Reads as posturing. "Ensemble" when "band" works. "Reading" when "playing" works. "The book" when "what we play" works. "A night of" when "we're playing" works. Use the working-musician word only when it's the right word; otherwise the plainer one always wins.
- **Empty claims with no specifics behind them.** "Eclectic," "unique," "one-of-a-kind," "incredible musicians." These signal nothing. If the sentence would survive being deleted, delete it.

## Sentence-level habits

- Active voice, concrete verbs.
- Short sentences and fragments are welcome. Vary length.
- **Never use the em dash character.** Reach for a comma, colon, semicolon, parentheses, or a full stop, whichever fits. This is a hard constraint from the global instructions and it overrides any stylistic preference; copy that arrives with one has to be fixed before it ships, so do not write it in the first place. An appositive comma covers most cases: "We're 8 Bit Mammoth, an 8-piece band playing video game music as funk and jazz."
- Italicize source titles when they appear (*Cowboy Bebop*, *The Legend of Zelda*). Title case piece names ("Tank!", "Stickerbrush Symphony"). Only when the user has asked for titles.
- Treat "8 Bit Mammoth" as a proper noun. Group size descriptors lowercase ("the eight-piece," "the twelve-piece").
- "We" / first-person collective is encouraged when it fits; it's warmer than third-person.

## Sanity check

Before returning copy, ask: does this sound like a person who's actually in the band wrote it? Could a friend forward this to someone without it feeling weird? Is there any sentence that's there just to sound professional, rather than to say something? If yes to that last one, cut it.

## Reference example: the voice landed here

User-accepted final, after several rounds of correction (Nash show, 8-piece, video-game focus, ticket-page paragraph):

> We're 8 Bit Mammoth, an 8-piece band playing video game music as funk and jazz. Horns out front, big rhythm section, and a stack of game soundtracks we all love, rearranged for the band. Some tunes go hard, some sit in a cool groove, but all hit that sweet nostalgia beat. Come hang with us at The Nash!

Why it works:
- First-person collective ("we're," "we all love"): feels like the band wrote it.
- "Stack of game soundtracks" / "rearranged for the band": concrete and casual, no writerly upgrade.
- "Some tunes go hard, some sit in a cool groove": vibe-words doing the descriptive work, no craft enumeration.
- "Hit that sweet nostalgia beat": names the emotional appeal directly, without irony or apology.
- Closing exclamation is welcome and warm, not hype.
- Zero specific titles, zero member names, zero defensive craft-listing, zero snobbery.

Future drafts should aim for this register unless the user asks for a different one (more formal bio, shorter caption, different show context, etc.).
